On Hold: designing the moment neobanks lose trust

Every neobank freezes accounts. The law requires it. What no bank has designed is everything around the freeze: what the person is told, when, and what still works. This is a working redesign of that experience, built in bunq's own design language, from 74 public complaints, and walked through screen by screen below.

Why this problem

The freeze isn't the failure. Everything around it is.

European regulators have spent three years fining challenger banks for weak financial-crime controls, and the industry reflex is always the same: monitor harder, freeze faster, ask more questions. Over-control freezes legitimate users, and every wrongly frozen account becomes a one-star review, a forum thread, and a prospect who abandons signup before ordering a card.

Why this matters most to bunq

bunq is built for people who live and work across borders. That is exactly the population whose financial lives look unusual to monitoring systems: income from several countries, irregular invoice patterns, transfers between their own accounts. bunq's best users are the ones most likely to get flagged. For bunq, the hold experience isn't an edge case. It's a core user journey wearing a support-ticket costume.

The redesign

Five components, one rule: never leave the person in the dark

1

A stepped case view

One screen owns the case: which of three steps you're on, what to do, and when the next update comes. A daily update lands even when nothing changed.

2

Profile-aware documents

Checklists matched to the user's life. Freelancers aren't asked for payslips. If a paper is impossible, a person reviews the alternative.

3

The transparency pattern

What we can tell you, and the one thing the law says we can't. Restricted information reads as regulation, not evasion.

4

Deadlines with consequences

The bank sets its own deadline and shows it. Miss it, and the make-good fires automatically: bank-set limits relax, the case jumps the queue, the miss is on screen.

5

Partial access by design

A review pauses the risk, not the whole life. What still works is spelled out, and limits lift on their own as stages complete.

Voice

No bank words

Compliance, KYC, verification, and escalation never appear. "We're checking your account" is the whole vocabulary. Anyone can follow it.

The architecture: five boxes

One door in, one hub, four rooms. Nothing is more than one tap from the hub, and each room answers exactly one question a worried person asks, in the order they ask it.

Where the law sits

None of this bends a rule. Most of it moves the bank closer to the rules it already has.

  • PSD2 art. 79

    Explaining a stopped payment is required, not just allowed. A payment provider must notify the user of a refusal and, where possible, the reasons, unless the law forbids it. A dead decline is closer to a breach than this design is.

  • Tipping-off

    The rules forbid revealing the trigger, a report, or an investigation. They say nothing against showing the stage, the timeline, or the next update. Everything on the case screen is process, not reason.

  • GDPR art. 22

    The human escape hatch on the document checklist is the right to human review of an automated decision with significant effects, in practice.

  • AMLR 2024

    Two carve-outs keep the deadline mechanic honest. Deadlines are commitments to communicate, not to release: an outside authority can extend a hold, and the bank cannot release funds early however late it is. So make-goods only ever touch bank-set limits, never legally frozen money, and the app says so plainly.

Humans stay in the plumbing where the law already puts them. They're never sold as a feature, and the design never competes with automation: it gives the assistant something true to say.

About this work
